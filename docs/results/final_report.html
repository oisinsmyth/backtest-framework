Backtest Framework · Final Report

Nothing worked, and that is the result

Every rule this project invented to add signal failed a fair test. Two things survived, and neither of them is signal. This is the record of how each one died, and of the machinery that made the deaths legible.

166 decision records 775 tests D189 · 22 Aug 2026

This report is dated. It covers D169–D189.

Written 22 August 2026, against 166 decision records and 775 tests. The programme did not stop there: it has since run to D539, and the repository now holds 745 records over 502 numbers and 2,054 tests. Roughly two thirds of the project post-dates this page.

Everything below is accurate as written and is not revised — each number was measured on the date this page carries. What is absent is everything after D189: the ETF pairs programme, D279’s look-ahead correction (about 93% of an apparent edge was a bug), the MACD ladder and the deflated-Sharpe hurdle that now fronts the README, the penny-exact vectorbt reconciliation, the intraday futures work, and the prop components ledger.

For the current state read the results index and the decision register. This page is the record of what the project looked like at D189, which is a different thing from a summary of what it is now.

What was being tested

A breakout book, and everything tried to improve it

A long book buys a 40-day high and exits on a 10-day low. A short book sells a 20-day low below the 200-day average, pays 10%/yr to borrow, and carries a mandatory stop. Both run walk-forward — fit on 252 days, trade the next 63, roll — so nothing is ever scored on data it was fitted on.

On top of that baseline, the project tested entry filters, six families of stop, two exit signatures, an ensemble, a cross-sectional portfolio, and finally a supply-and-demand terrain model. Each addition was registered before it ran, scored against a stated bar, and kept or dropped on that bar alone.

The discipline mattered more than any of the ideas. Predictions were committed to git before the run that would score them, so a wrong call is dated and cannot be quietly rewritten. Nine studies were pre-registered this way, stating twenty-three hypotheses between them. Close to half turned out wrong — a record that is only meaningful because the falsifications are still in the repository, unedited, above the results that refuted them.

Act one: the rules

Fifty-seven registered configurations, none surviving contact with new instruments

Four entry filters were tested and all four removed good trades along with bad. Six stop families were swept; three failed outright and the rest split by symbol, which is a coin flip wearing a result's clothes.

The most instructive failure was E1 — a rule that exits a breakout which immediately falls back inside the channel it broke. It cleared its bar five times: the long book at two settings, the short book at two settings, and the combined book with both bootstrap intervals excluding zero. Five independent passes.

Then it was run on 62 coins, a universe screened mechanically to include the ones that failed — 41 that fell 90%+ and never recovered, two delisted.

Five “independent” passes were five readings of two unusually favourable price series. Nothing about the statistics was wrong; the sample was.

Act two: the portfolio

The one thing that worked, and how far it got

Every result up to this point was a single-instrument book, or a cross-section of single-instrument books averaged afterwards. Averaging Sharpes is not the same as earning the Sharpe of an average. Holding all 62 books at once had never been tried.

It worked. The long book went from a median single-coin Sharpe of +0.437 to a portfolio Sharpe of +1.277, with maximum drawdown falling from a 48% average to 28.8%. Nothing else tested moved a number like that.

It was also the least surprising result in the project, because it is arithmetic rather than a discovery — and it is the only lever tried that operates on the return distribution rather than on one book's decisions.

Three debts were then paid, in order.

The finding inside the failure

Going in, I predicted the diversification lift would reproduce, calling it arithmetic, not a market claim. It did not. The ETF portfolio scored −0.273 against a median single ETF of −0.177. The lift did not fail to appear — it reversed.

The arithmetic is exactly why. Sharpe is mean ÷ σ. Averaging partially-independent books does shrink σ — but it divides a mean by that smaller σ. Positive mean: shrinking σ raises the Sharpe. Negative mean: it makes the Sharpe worse.

Diversification is a magnifier with the sign of the expectancy. It does not create edge; it concentrates whichever sign is already there.

That correction applies backwards without changing a single number: the crypto lift was real, and it was real because those books had positive expectancy — not because averaging is free.

Act three: the terrain model

A supply-and-demand map, and the null test that closed it

The last phase specified a terrain model: sensors estimating where resting supply and demand sit relative to price, fused into a field that would gate entries and modulate exits. Its own plan carried a stop condition — if the lead sensor cannot beat randomly placed levels, the programme stops rather than hoping a weaker sensor rescues it.

The lead sensor is a volume profile: a histogram of dollars traded at each price over a rolling window, from which high-volume nodes are extracted as levels. The test asks one question. Does price behave differently at those levels than at the same number of levels scattered at random over the same range?

Sixteen configurations were run — two symbols, two lookbacks, two bucket widths, two touch bands. Not one beat its null on any metric. On the reversal statistic the entire model rests on, the real levels sit below the null median on both symbols.

The programme stopped there, by its own stated condition. The lead sensor was the strongest of the three on the specification's own assessment; the second is marginal by design and the third has, in the spec's words, zero independent validation in the literature. A programme whose best sensor cannot beat noise is not rescued by its weaker ones.

The control that caught me twice

The harness had to pass two synthetic checks: find levels that were deliberately planted, and find nothing in a pure random walk. The false-positive check passed first time across three seeds.

The positive control failed twice, and both times my test fixture was wrong. A drift term of −0.9 × gap pushes price toward a level — a magnet, not a wall — so the harness correctly reported the planted level as worse than chance. Flipping the sign built a wall price could never return to: one touch in 1,500 bars.

The harness caught both errors before either could reach a result. That is what a positive control is for, and it is worth more than a clean first pass.

What survived

Two properties, neither of which is signal

Reduced drawdown. The one claim that held in every test, including the ETF cross-section where everything else inverted: 6.1% against the basket's 33.4%. It should be read for what it is — a book invested a fraction of the time has a small drawdown for the same reason it has a small return. At realistic costs on ETFs the strategy returns −0.5%, which is nearly cash, and cash also has a small drawdown.

Diversification. Real, arithmetic, and — as the ETF run showed — carrying the sign of the expectancy rather than creating one.

Meanwhile the deflated Sharpe on the long book reads 0.9998 on BTC against a pool of 30 configurations, which sounds like a triumph and is not. A deflated Sharpe deflates against a trial pool, not against a benchmark. At the point where the portfolio and a passive basket both score +1.161, that number would still be high. It measures crypto beta surviving a multiplicity correction, not skill surviving one.

The bugs are the other half of the report

Seven real defects, and what each one taught

Every one of these was found by a guard, an assertion, or an implausible number — not by inspection. Several would have flattered the results had they gone unnoticed.

  • D181 The ensemble weighted itself with whole-sample volatility. The structural look-ahead guard makes this impossible for a strategy and does nothing for analytics built on strategy output. That boundary had never been written down. The missing test was one line: the weight at bar t must not move when returns at t or later change.
  • D187 Crypto volume is quoted in dollars; the impact model read it as coins. Every market-impact charge was off by √price — understated 148× on BTC, overstated 40× on a sub-cent coin, in the same run. It produced two results that looked like findings and were artifacts. A units error is invisible to every other check here: the name of a field is not a contract, the parameter that enforces it is.
  • D184 A benchmark returned +102,682,123%, from one bar. A single coin printed a 3.4-million-percent day on a price-scale defect. Chasing the absurd number rather than quoting it exposed an empty corporate-actions file, four scripts declining to load it, and a validator override nobody had inspected. An implausible number in a throwaway comparison is worth more attention than a plausible one in a result.
  • D186 Volumes reached the policy screen and never reached the run. Harmless for as long as nothing downstream wanted them; a loud failure the moment a cost model needed them, which is the only reason it surfaced.
  • D176 A dead diagnostic had its direction reversed — it would have reported profitable moves as losses. Never called, so nothing was published from it. Dead code is unreviewed code wearing the appearance of a delivered requirement.
  • D177 A report printed an impossibility — a cumulative rate that fell as its window grew. The arithmetic could not be true, which is the only reason the counting bug behind it was visible at all.
  • D169 A headline claim was a measurement artifact. “Four of four stop exits gapped through by 38.5%” came from inferring stop exits from price rather than recording them. The true count was one.

What the discipline was worth

The parts that earned their cost

Pre-registration. Nine studies, twenty-three hypotheses, every one committed before the run that scored it. Close to half were wrong, and the wrong ones are the useful ones — twice I identified a real mechanism and drew the wrong consequence from it, both times by reasoning about a numerator and forgetting its denominator. That pattern is only visible because the predictions are dated.

The every-symbol rule. Keep only what improves on both instruments, because one of two is a coin flip. It killed several rules that a one-symbol reading would have promoted.

The failure universe. A cross-section screened to include assets that died, not the ones that survived to be worth studying. It is the only test in the project that ever caught anything, and it caught both rules put to it.

Loud failures over silent defaults. Missing volume raises rather than costing zero. An unknown unit raises rather than guessing. An unavailable sensor returns nothing rather than an empty density. Most of the bugs above surfaced because something refused to proceed quietly.

Walk-forward, a trial registry, deflated Sharpe, block bootstraps, pre-registration — all correct, all applied, and all satisfied by a rule that does not work. Every one controls for error within a sample. None can detect that the sample is the problem.

What remains untested

The assumption no data on hand can reach

Both cross-sections — 62 coins and 57 ETFs — span 2015 to 2024. A different asset class is not a different era. Nothing here tests the strategy against a period unlike the one it was built in, and that is now the deepest untested assumption in the project. It cannot be fixed with any file in the repository.

Three smaller gaps are documented and deliberately unfixed: there is no margin-call or liquidation model, so short accounts have passed −100% and kept trading; market impact is calibrated on full-sample volume, a mild look-ahead in cost parameters though never in the signal; and inverse-volatility weighting pays a book for being absent, handing the losing leg 70% of the risk budget precisely because it barely trades.

The terrain result closes one sensor on daily bars on two instruments. A sharper map built from real exchange intraday volume is a different measurement — the available hourly series reports zero volume on half its bars, which is why the test ran on daily. The harness is kept: it passed both controls and can test any future sensor.

The honest summary

One paragraph, no hedging

A breakout book on crypto, held across many instruments at once, returns roughly what an equal-weight basket of the same instruments returns, at about a third of its drawdown, with a small edge over that basket that survives realistic trading costs and dies somewhere around $66M of capital. That edge is a property of one asset class in one decade. On a different asset class, with no refitting, it inverts. Every rule invented to improve the book failed a fair out-of-sample test, and the supply-and-demand model built to explain where price stalls could not distinguish its own levels from randomly placed ones.

The project produced no tradable edge. It produced a harness that reliably refuses to confirm what it is pointed at, a documented record of the ideas that do not work, and a short list of ways a quantitative result can be wrong while every statistic in it is computed correctly.

A result measured on a favourable sample is not a result about the world. Learning that cost every idea in the project, and it was still the cheapest thing here.
