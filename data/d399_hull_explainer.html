D399 · how the convex hull construction works

The Hull as a Trendline

A support line that price never breaks is not like a convex hull edge — it is one. That identity is the whole idea, and it also contains the trap that makes the obvious version of it the worst rule in the family.

1 · Stretch a rubber band under the lows

Plot every bar as a point at (bar index, log low) and push a taut band up from below. It touches some lows and misses the rest. Those touch points are the hull vertices, and each straight run between two of them is a hull edge.

Every hull edge is a support line no low has ever pierced — that is what the band being taut means. And it is the tightest such line, because the band is pulled as high as it will go. Below, GME's 260 bars give a lower hull of just 8 vertices; the upper hull of the highs gives 8 more.

2 · The trap: today is always a vertex

The newest bar is always on its own hull. Nothing to its right exists to pull the band past it, so the last point is a vertex by construction, every single bar.

So the edge ending at today is the steepest line back from today's low — and it re-pivots every bar. Taking "the hull edge at the most recent vertex" as the trendline scored 0.086, the worst rule tried. Here is why, at three different bars:

The last edge spans one bar every time. It is not a trendline; it is a segment joining yesterday's low to today's.

3 · The fix: use an edge that has had time to settle

Take instead the most recent edge whose right-hand vertex is at least 5 bars old — an edge the band has already committed to and not revised. Same hull, one step back from the front.

That rule scored 0.513 against 0.086 for the naive one, and it won at every window length and every gate, so it is a family rather than one lucky cell.

4 · Why it is a good model, and where it still fails

It matches what a person actually does. A drawn trendline rests on two wicks with nothing poking through it — which is the definition of a hull edge. That is why this family more than doubled the incumbent construction's score without being tuned into shape.

But it does not clear its own null. Rotating the fitted gradients in time — all 259 offsets, so the p95 has zero standard error — gives p95 0.534 against the observed 0.513. Nineteen percent of rotations beat it. The benchmark cannot yet tell this apart from a construction that gets the spread of slopes right and puts them anywhere.

And it fails at the window's start, where a 252-bar hull is still anchored on bars from before the window began — agreement 0.13 and 0.17 on the first two drawn lines, against 0.88, 0.84 and 0.68 on the crash and the drift. That part is the oracle gap, not a fixable bug.
